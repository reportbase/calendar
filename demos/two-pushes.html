Two pushes, one turn

The calendar dial pushes along one line, so its pushes commute. Here the reader can push any way. Each push is a boost along the reader's own axes, the way a ship's controls work. Press and drag on the disc: the drag from where you pressed is the push, ahead and to the side as the compass shows them. The blue point is the reader's velocity in the disc of velocities (rest in the middle, light at the rim). Push out and come back to rest, and the reader's frame comes home turned, by the area its velocity path enclosed.

Velocities: the Poincaré disc, the path pushed, the reader's compass

The list seen from the reader's own frame

What it shows. A loop of pushes that returns to rest turns the reader's frame by minus the hyperbolic area the loop enclosed in velocity space (Thomas precession; Gauss–Bonnet at curvature −1). Pushes along one line enclose no area and never turn: that is why the one-dimensional dial has no turn. The turn is a reading, an angle with no unit and no convention. It records the path taken and depends on the loop. Very small loops turn as the square of their size; larger ones faster, since hyperbolic area outgrows the square (halving the page's square divides its turn by 4.86, not 4). For a two-dimensional dial over a list, the turn is either a record worth showing or a drift to cancel, by pushing home along a line or by adding the opposite turn back.

Classical. Thomas 1926; Wigner 1939; the area law by Gauss–Bonnet in the velocity space of Varićak 1910. The page checks it live: when the velocity returns to rest it compares the turn with the area enclosed.
